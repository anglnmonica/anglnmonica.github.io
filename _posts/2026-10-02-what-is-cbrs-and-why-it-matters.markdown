If you've worked anywhere near private wireless networks in the last few years, you've heard the acronym CBRS. Here's what it actually means, and why it matters more than most spectrum bands ever have.

## The problem CBRS solves

Wireless spectrum — the airwaves that carry cellular signals — is expensive and tightly controlled. Historically, only major carriers like Verizon or AT&T could afford to license it, which meant only they could build cellular networks. A hospital, a warehouse, or a university that wanted its own private LTE or 5G network had no legal way to get spectrum of its own.

CBRS (Citizens Broadband Radio Service) changes that. It's a band of spectrum — 3.5 GHz — that the U.S. government opened up for shared use, instead of selling it exclusively to one company.

## How the sharing works

CBRS splits access into three tiers:

- **Incumbent Access** — the U.S. Navy and a handful of existing users get top priority and can't be interfered with.
- **Priority Access License (PAL)** — companies can bid for guaranteed access in a specific area, similar to a traditional license, but at a much smaller and cheaper scale.
- **General Authorized Access (GAA)** — anyone can use this tier for free, as long as they don't interfere with the tiers above it.

A system called a SAS (Spectrum Access System) automatically manages all of this in real time, clearing devices to transmit only when it's safe to do so.

## Why this matters for enterprises

This tiered structure means a manufacturing plant, a port, or a large retail campus can deploy its own private LTE or 5G network — without needing to buy spectrum from a carrier. That private network can be tuned specifically for the site: dedicated capacity for a fleet of autonomous forklifts, guaranteed low latency for robotics, or reliable coverage in a steel-and-concrete warehouse where Wi-Fi often struggles.

This is the foundation a lot of today's "private 5G" industry is built on. When you hear about a company's own in-building 5G network, there's a good chance CBRS is what made it possible.

## The trade-offs

CBRS isn't free of complexity. The GAA tier is uncoordinated, meaning multiple companies in the same area can compete for the same channels, and performance can vary. Enterprises serious about guaranteed performance usually pursue a PAL license instead, which adds cost and a bidding process.

Still, for the first time, spectrum access became something a business could plan for and budget — not something only a telecom giant could touch.
